When your game adds ten points to your score, your C P U can do the actual math in a single clock tick, a fifth of a billionth of a second. Or it can sit and wait for hundreds of ticks while nothing happens. Today we follow those ten points through one processor core, cycle by cycle, and see exactly where the time goes.

It starts as one line of code: score equals score plus ten. Your C P U can't read that. A compiler turned it into machine instructions long before you hit play. Add ten to the score sitting in memory. Then compare it with the high score, and jump somewhere else if you just beat it. Each instruction sits at an address in memory, and a register called the program counter always points at the next one to run.

Everything inside the core moves to the beat of a clock. At five gigahertz, that clock ticks five billion times every second, and today's fastest desktop chips boost even a little higher, to around five point seven. At five gigahertz, one tick lasts just two tenths of a nanosecond. That's so short that light itself only travels about six centimeters before the next tick arrives. Every step our ten points take has to fit inside these tiny slices of time.

Step one is fetch. The front of the core reads the program counter and pulls the instruction bytes out of a small, very fast memory right next to it, the level one instruction cache, about thirty two kilobytes. A modern core doesn't fetch one instruction at a time. It grabs a whole chunk of upcoming code every tick, because it's already planning several steps ahead.

Step two is decode. The instructions your game was compiled into are complicated, and they come in different lengths. So the decoder chops each one into simple pieces called micro-ops. Our add-to-the-score instruction can become three of them: load the old value, do the addition, and store the result. To save effort, the core keeps recently decoded micro-ops in a micro-op cache, so a loop that runs every frame doesn't have to be decoded again and again.

Step three is execute, and this is where registers and the A L U come in. Registers are the core's tiny scratchpads, holding the numbers it's working on right now. Your program sees sixteen general purpose registers, each sixty four bits wide. The load micro-op copies the score into one of them. Then the A L U, the arithmetic logic unit, adds ten. A simple add like this takes exactly one clock cycle, and a core like A M D's Zen four has four of these units, so it can do four adds in the same tick.

Now here's the trick that makes it fast. Fetch, decode and execute aren't done one after another for each instruction. They're a pipeline, like an assembly line. While our add is executing, the next instruction is being decoded, and the one after that is being fetched. A single instruction doesn't finish any sooner, but with every stage busy, finished work comes out of the end of the line every tick instead of every few ticks.

Modern cores go further and run instructions out of order. If the load is stuck waiting for the score to arrive, the core doesn't freeze. It looks ahead for work that doesn't depend on the score, like moving your character or playing a sound, and runs that first. A structure called the reorder buffer keeps track of everything in flight, three hundred and twenty micro-ops on Zen four, and four hundred and forty eight on the newer Zen five. Results are then retired in the original order, so your program never notices the shuffle.

So where is the score, and how long is the wait? If it was used a moment ago, it's in the level one data cache, about four cycles away. If not, the core checks level two, around fourteen cycles. Then the shared level three cache, around fifty cycles. And if it has to come all the way from your RAM sticks, that's roughly seventy nanoseconds, or about three hundred and fifty cycles at five gigahertz. That's the hundreds of ticks of waiting from the start, and it's why cache size matters so much in games.

Next comes the comparison with your high score, and that means a branch, a fork in the road. The core can't afford to wait and see which way it goes, so a branch predictor guesses, based on what this same branch did before. The core starts running the guessed path right away. Most of the time the guess is right and no time is lost. When it's wrong, all that speculative work gets thrown out, the pipeline is flushed, and fetching restarts on the correct path. Measurements on recent cores put that penalty at roughly fifteen to twenty cycles.

Put it all together and you get the number that really decides speed, I P C, or instructions per cycle. Clock speed tells you how many ticks happen each second. I P C tells you how much useful work gets finished in each tick. A wide core with a good predictor and big caches can retire several instructions every cycle, and a Zen five core can feed up to eight micro-ops into its out-of-order engine in a single tick. But in games, cache misses and wrong guesses keep the real average much lower than the maximum, which is why two chips at the same gigahertz can perform very differently.

So let's replay our ten points. Fetched from the instruction cache, decoded into three micro-ops, the score loaded into a register, added in one cycle by the A L U, stored back, and the high score check guessed by the predictor, all overlapped with dozens of other instructions in the pipeline. With the score in cache, that whole trip takes just a few nanoseconds. Your game does this billions of times every second.
