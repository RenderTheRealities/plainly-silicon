You double-click a game. Three seconds later, you're on the main menu. In those three seconds, every single part inside your PC did a job, and most people couldn't name half of them. So let's follow that one click through the whole machine, part by part. By the end, you'll know what every component does, which ones actually matter for speed, and where people waste money.

Start at the wall. Your outlet sends out alternating current, around 120 volts in North America. Nothing inside your PC can use that. So the first part that wakes up is the power supply. Its whole job is translation: it turns wall power into steady, low-voltage direct current, mostly 12 volts, plus 5 and 3.3 volts for smaller parts. Every other component depends on it, which is why a cheap, unrated power supply is the one corner you should never cut. When it fails, it can take other parts with it. Look for an efficiency rating like 80 Plus Bronze or better, and a wattage with some headroom above what your parts draw.

That power flows into the motherboard. Think of the motherboard as the city the other parts live in. It doesn't do the thinking. It provides the roads. Copper traces connect the processor to the memory, the graphics card, and the storage, and the board decides how fast those roads can run. It also holds a small chip with firmware called UEFI, the modern version of the BIOS. That's the first code that runs when you press the power button. It checks that everything is present, then hands control to your operating system.

Now, back to your click. Your mouse sends a tiny signal through USB, and the motherboard delivers it to the brain of the system: the CPU, or central processing unit. The CPU runs instructions one tiny step at a time, billions of times per second. That's what the gigahertz number means. A 4 gigahertz chip ticks through about four billion cycles every second. Modern CPUs also have multiple cores, which are like several workers sharing one desk. Your click becomes a list of instructions: find the game, load it, start it. The CPU starts working through that list immediately.

But the CPU has a problem. The game isn't anywhere near it. It's sitting on your storage drive. Storage is the long-term memory of your PC. It keeps everything when the power is off: your operating system, your games, your photos. There are two main kinds. A hard drive stores data on spinning magnetic platters, read by an arm that physically moves into place. It's cheap and roomy, but slow, usually around 150 to 200 megabytes per second. A solid state drive has no moving parts at all. It stores data in flash memory chips. A good NVMe SSD, the kind that slots straight into the motherboard, can read thousands of megabytes per second. That's why the single biggest upgrade for an old, slow PC is usually swapping a hard drive for an SSD.

So the game files start flowing off the SSD. But they don't go straight to the CPU. Storage, even fast storage, is still far too slow for the processor to work from directly. So the files land in RAM. RAM is short-term memory: a fast workspace the CPU can grab from in nanoseconds. Here's the key difference. RAM forgets everything the moment you cut the power. Storage remembers. RAM is the desk you're working at. Storage is the filing cabinet across the room. More RAM means a bigger desk, so you can keep more open at once without walking back to the cabinet. But once your desk is big enough for what you do, adding more doesn't make anything faster. For most gaming right now, 16 to 32 gigabytes is the comfortable range. Past that, you're usually paying for desk space you'll never use.

Even RAM is too slow for the CPU's taste. So the processor keeps its own tiny, extremely fast memory built right into the chip, called cache. It's measured in megabytes, not gigabytes, but it's so close to the cores that the CPU checks it first for anything it needs. Cache, then RAM, then storage. Each step is bigger, cheaper, and slower than the one before.

With the game loaded, it's time to draw the main menu, and this is where the CPU hands off to a specialist. The GPU, or graphics processing unit, lives on your graphics card. A CPU has a handful of very smart cores. A GPU has thousands of simpler ones. That sounds like a downgrade, but drawing an image is a perfect job for a crowd. Your screen has millions of pixels, and most of them can be calculated at the same time, independently. So the GPU splits the frame into a massive number of small tasks and runs them all in parallel. The graphics card also has its own memory, called VRAM, where it keeps textures and models close at hand. In games, the GPU usually matters more for frame rate than the CPU does. That's why it's often the most expensive part in a gaming build.

All that work produces heat. A CPU or GPU under load can turn well over a hundred watts of electricity into heat, in a space smaller than a cracker. So the next part is cooling. A cooler sits on the CPU, with a thin layer of thermal paste filling the microscopic gaps between them. Heat moves into the cooler's metal fins or liquid, and fans push it away. Your graphics card has its own cooler built in. And the case fans move fresh air through the whole system. If cooling falls behind, the chips protect themselves by slowing down. That's called thermal throttling, and it's a common hidden reason a PC feels slower in summer.

Finally, the case. It's not just a box. It sets the airflow path, protects the parts, and decides what fits inside: how long a graphics card, how tall a cooler, how many drives.

So let's replay that click. The power supply feeds clean power. The motherboard connects everything. The CPU reads your click and starts the instructions. The SSD hands over the game files. RAM holds them close. Cache feeds the CPU even faster. The GPU draws the menu across millions of pixels at once. And the coolers keep it all from overheating. Three seconds, eight parts, all working together.
