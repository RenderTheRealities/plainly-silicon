One tester left a gaming PC alone for six months, then cleaned out the dust. The processor ran 11 degrees cooler in games, and the graphics card about 4 degrees cooler, with nothing else changed. In the next few minutes we'll clean one dusty PC together, in the right order, and I'll show you the three ways people break hardware while doing it.

Here's why dust matters at all. Every fan in your case pulls air across thin metal fins, and the heat leaves with that air. Dust settles on the fins and in the filters like a felt blanket. Less air gets through, so the chips get hotter. Modern processors and graphics cards don't just get warm and carry on. They protect themselves by lowering their boost clocks, which means fewer frames and louder fans. So a dusty PC is often a slower PC, not just a dirtier one.

Our PC is a normal tower that's been running for a year in a bedroom. Before we touch it, shut it down from Windows. Then flip the switch on the back of the power supply to the off position, the side marked with a circle, and pull the power cord out. With the cord out, press and hold the power button for about 30 seconds. That drains the little bit of charge still sitting in the system. Now carry the PC somewhere with a hard floor and some airflow. A garage, a balcony or a table by an open window is perfect, because the dust is about to go everywhere.

The first way people break a PC is static. Your body can carry a charge you never feel, and a spark you can't even see can damage a chip. So stand on a hard floor, not carpet, and touch the bare metal of the case before you reach inside. Touch it again now and then while you work. An antistatic wrist strap clipped to the case is even better, and it's cheap. This is also why a household vacuum cleaner should never go inside the case. Its plastic nozzle builds up static as air rushes through it, and it's easy to knock a small part off the board.

Start with the easy wins: the dust filters. Most cases have a mesh filter at the front, one under the power supply, and often one on top. They usually slide out or pull off with magnets. Here the vacuum is fine, because the filter is out of the PC. Most mesh filters can also be rinsed under the tap. If you wash one, let it dry completely before it goes back in. Water and electronics only mix when you give them a chance, so don't rush this part.

Now the compressed air, and the second way people break things. Point the air at a fan and it spins like a pinwheel, far faster than it was ever built to run. That can wear out its bearings or even crack a blade. So hold every fan still with a finger or a plastic tool before you blow on it. Use short bursts, not one long blast, and keep the nozzle about 5 to 10 centimetres away. Keep the can upright, too. Tip it sideways or upside down and it can spit freezing liquid propellant onto your parts.

The order matters. Work from the top of the case down, so loose dust falls toward the bottom and out, not onto parts you've already cleaned. On the processor cooler and the graphics card, blow from the side where the air normally leaves the heatsink. That pushes dust back out the way it came in, instead of packing it deeper into the fins. Clean the case fans, then the cooler, then the graphics card, then the floor of the case. Ten minutes of this and you'll see the difference with your own eyes.

The third way people break things is going too deep. Never open the power supply. Its capacitors can hold a dangerous charge even after it's unplugged, and there's nothing inside you need to clean. Just blow through its vents from the outside. Don't take the graphics card apart either. And don't take the processor cooler off just to dust it, because if you lift it, the thermal paste underneath has to be cleaned off and replaced. On the motherboard and the memory, use air only. Brushes can snag tiny parts there.

Some dust turns into sticky grime, especially near smokers or kitchens. For that, put a little isopropyl alcohol on a cotton swab or a microfibre cloth, wipe, and let it evaporate. Never spray anything directly into the case. Skip household cleaners with bleach or ammonia, and skip paper towels, which shed fibres and scratch. And wear a simple dust mask. A year of dust is not something you want in your lungs.

Before you put the side panel back on, plug the PC in and turn it on. Look at every fan. Each one should spin, and none should rattle or wobble. A fan that stays still might have a loose cable you knocked while cleaning. Then close it up and play the same game you played before. Watch the temperatures with a free monitoring tool and compare. For that tester, this is where the 11 degrees showed up.

To keep it clean for longer, check the filters every month or two, and do a full clean like this every three to six months. With pets, smokers or a dusty room, make it every two to three months. Keep the tower off the carpet if you can. And set your fans for slight positive pressure, which means more fans pulling air in than pushing it out. A common setup is two or three intake fans at the front, each behind a filter, and one exhaust fan at the back. Then most air enters through the filters, and less dust sneaks in through every gap.
