Same game, same PC, same save file. In one PCWorld test, Cyberpunk twenty seventy-seven loaded in 6 seconds from an S S D and 63 seconds from a hard drive. Yet in TechSpot's testing, a drive with several times the top speed of another loaded Starfield less than a second faster. So let's follow one load screen through every kind of drive and find out exactly where your waiting time goes.

First, what does a load screen actually ask for? Not one big file. The game needs textures, models, sounds and map data, often thousands of pieces scattered across the drive. Each piece has to be found, read, and then unpacked by your processor before the world can appear. That gives a drive two very different jobs. Reading one long stream of data is called sequential speed. Finding lots of small, scattered pieces depends on how long each lookup takes. Keep both in mind, because each drive type wins and loses in a different place.

Start with the hard drive. Inside is a metal platter spinning seventy-two hundred times a minute, and an arm with a read head that swings across it. To fetch a piece of data, the arm has to move to the right ring, which takes about 8 to 10 milliseconds. Then it waits for the right spot to spin underneath, about 4 more. So every scattered lookup costs roughly 12 milliseconds. Once it's in place, reading is decent. A current Seagate BarraCuda is rated at up to 190 megabytes per second, and that's on the fast outer edge of the platter.

Now run our load screen on it. Twelve milliseconds sounds tiny, but it means a hard drive can only handle around a hundred to two hundred scattered reads per second. A level that needs thousands of them turns into a queue, and the arm spends most of its time travelling instead of reading. That's how you get 63 seconds in Cyberpunk. In TechSpot's tests, Kingdom Come Deliverance Two took over four minutes to load from a hard drive, and the game itself warns you not to use one. Worse, modern games keep streaming data while you play. On a slow hard drive, TechSpot saw Stalker Two load with missing pieces of the world.

Next, the Sata S S D. There's no arm and no spinning platter. Data lives in flash memory chips, and a controller reads from several chips at the same time. A lookup takes around a tenth of a millisecond instead of twelve, so scattered reads stop being a queue and become a crowd that's served all at once. The catch is the cable. Sata was designed for hard drives. Its link runs at 6 gigabits per second, but encoding overhead leaves about 600 megabytes per second of real data, and Sata drives top out around 550.

So what happens to our load screen? This is the big jump. The hard drive's 12 millisecond penalty disappears, and the wait collapses. In TechSpot's testing, reloading a Cyberpunk save took about the same time on every S S D they tried, Sata included, while hard drives were up to nearly four times slower. For a lot of games, the moment you leave spinning disks behind, storage stops being the problem.

Then comes N V M e. Instead of a Sata cable, the drive talks over P C I e lanes, the same kind of high-speed link a graphics card uses, usually four lanes wide. Each P C I e generation roughly doubles the speed of a lane. A P C I e three drive like Samsung's 970 EVO Plus is rated at 3,500 megabytes per second. A P C I e four drive like the 990 Pro reaches about 7,450. And P C I e five drives like the 9100 Pro are rated at up to 14,800. N V M e can also keep many queues of requests open at once, a perfect fit for a game asking for thousands of pieces.

So why doesn't a drive twenty-five times faster than Sata load games twenty-five times faster? Because once the data arrives, your processor still has to unpack it, build the world, and set up the level. The bottleneck moves from the drive to the C P U. In TechSpot's tests, Starfield loaded less than a second apart on P C I e five and P C I e three. F1 twenty-five was identical across every N V M e drive, and Red Dead Redemption Two showed no difference between any S S Ds at all. Back in 2018, Legit Reviews even found a budget N V M e drive that was slower than a good Sata one.

But Sata is starting to show its age, and you can see where. Games built for modern consoles, which all have fast N V M e storage, are designed to pull in data constantly. In TechSpot's results, Ratchet and Clank Rift Apart loaded in just 1.3 seconds on a P C I e drive, while Sata drives were nearly five times slower. In Spider-Man Two, Sata was up to nearly four times slower in the worst cases, and The Last of Us Part Two took at least 60 percent longer.

Some of those games use Microsoft's Direct Storage. It lets a game batch thousands of small requests and hand them to the N V M e drive's queues in one go, instead of one at a time. Version one point one also moves decompression from the processor to the graphics card. In PCWorld's tests of Forspoken, the first Direct Storage game, one scene took 7.4 seconds on a Sata S S D and 1.9 seconds on a P C I e four drive. This is the one place where a faster drive can really change the clock, and only in games built for it.

So let's race our load screen one last time. The hard drive is still finding pieces long after everyone else is playing, sometimes for minutes. The Sata S S D finishes close to the leaders in most games, and falls behind in newer console-style ones. Any N V M e drive gets you nearly all the benefit. P C I e five rarely helps games, and TechSpot found it costs at least 40 percent more than P C I e four. One quick check before you buy: M point two is just a shape, so make sure your slot actually supports N V M e.
