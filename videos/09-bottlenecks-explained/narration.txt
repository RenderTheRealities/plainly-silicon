You can put a top-end graphics card into a PC and get only a little over half the frame rate it's capable of, because the processor feeding it can't keep up. By the end of this video you'll know how to tell, in about a minute, whether your CPU or your GPU is the part setting your frame rate, and what to do about it without spending anything.

A bottleneck is just the narrowest part of a pipe. Water can only flow as fast as the tightest section allows, no matter how wide the rest of the pipe is. A game works the same way. For every frame, the processor goes first. It runs the game logic, moves the characters, works out the physics, and writes a list of drawing instructions. The graphics card takes that list and turns it into pixels. Whichever of the two takes longer per frame sets your frame rate. The other one simply waits.

That means every PC has a bottleneck, all the time. It isn't a fault, and it isn't something you can get rid of. If your graphics card is the slower stage, that's actually the ideal case, because you're getting everything you paid for out of the most expensive part in the box. So the useful question isn't whether you have a bottleneck. It's which part it is, and by how much.

To see this clearly, let's follow one real test from start to finish. The hardware site TechSpot took a budget Intel Core i3 13100, a four core chip, and paired it with three graphics cards in turn: an RTX 3060, an RTX 3080, and an RTX 4090. Then they ran the same tests with a Core i9 13900K for comparison. The game was Watch Dogs Legion at very high settings. Same game, same settings. Only the parts change.

Start with the RTX 3060 at 1080p. With this card, the expensive Core i9 is just 3 percent faster than the little Core i3. Three percent. The graphics card is the narrow section of the pipe, so it doesn't matter how wide the processor side is. Both CPUs finish their share of every frame early, and then they wait. If you owned the Core i3 and this card, buying a new processor would be money wasted.

Now swap in the RTX 3080, a much faster card. Suddenly the Core i9 is 50 percent faster. Nothing about either processor changed. The graphics card simply got wide enough that the Core i3 became the narrowest point. Same CPU, different bottleneck. This is why a question like "is my processor good enough" has no answer on its own. It always depends on what it's paired with, and on what you're asking it to run.

With the RTX 4090, the gap gets extreme. The Core i3 stops at 102 frames per second. The Core i9 reaches 186, which is 82 percent faster. And here's the part that surprises people. Even at 4K, the i3 still holds the 4090 back to 99 frames per second, while the i9 gets 149. Going from 1080p to 4K means four times as many pixels, and the Core i3's result barely moved, from 102 to 99. That flat line is the fingerprint of a CPU limit.

Why doesn't resolution matter to the processor? Because its job for each frame is the same at any resolution. The same characters walk around, the same physics runs, and the same list of drawing instructions gets written. Only the graphics card's job grows with more pixels. So if raising the resolution barely changes your frame rate, the graphics card was waiting and the processor was the wall. What does load the processor is the world itself: crowds of characters, moving vehicles, physics, long draw distances, and ray tracing.

Here's how to check your own PC. Turn on a performance overlay, like the one in MSI Afterburner or your graphics driver, and watch GPU usage while you play. If it stays close to 100 percent, your graphics card is the limit, and that's fine. If it sits well below that, and no frame cap or V-Sync is holding it back, your processor is the likely limit. Don't be fooled when total CPU usage looks low. Many games lean on just a few cores, so a few cores can be flat out while the overall number looks relaxed. That's why a six core Ryzen 5600X performs about the same as a sixteen core 5950X in most games.

You may have seen bottleneck calculator websites that give a single percentage for any CPU and GPU pair. Skip them. Nobody has tested every combination, and the real number changes with every game, resolution and setting. You just watched the same Core i3 go from a 3 percent gap to an 82 percent gap. Real benchmarks of the games you actually play tell you far more. It's also why reviewers test processors at 1080p with the fastest graphics card they can get. It takes the GPU out of the way, so you see the processor's real ceiling, which is the number you'll run into after your next graphics upgrade.

So what do you do about it? If you're GPU limited, lower the heaviest settings or turn on upscaling, which renders fewer pixels. If you're CPU limited, you get a small gift. Your graphics card has spare room, so you can raise resolution, textures or lighting quality and your frame rate will barely drop. Then turn down the settings that load the processor, like crowd density and view distance. If your frame rate is already above your monitor's refresh rate, a frame cap smooths things out and saves power. Only when the processor holds you well below the frame rate you want is a CPU upgrade worth the money.

So remember the pipe. The narrowest part sets the flow, and the cheapest fix always starts with finding that part before you buy anything.
