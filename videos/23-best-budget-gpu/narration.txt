The best value gaming graphics card this Black Friday isn't made by Nvidia or AMD. And one of the cards you'll see in a lot of deal banners this week belongs in the skip tier. We're going to rank every budget graphics card you can actually buy at the end of November 2026, using one number: how many dollars you pay for each frame. By the end, you'll know which card to buy, and the exact price where you should walk away.

First, why this year is strange. Every card in this video sells for more today than it did at launch, some for more than double. AI data centers are buying memory as fast as it can be made, and chipmakers have shifted production toward the memory those servers use. That leaves less of the memory gaming cards need. Nvidia has reportedly raised prices to its board partners several times this year, and AMD reportedly told partners to expect about ten percent more for the last quarter. So launch prices are history. We'll use what US stores were actually listing in the middle of November.

Here's how the ranking works. Every card gets a speed score at 1080p, where the RTX 50 60 counts as 100. The scores come from averaging several independent reviews, and they're rounded, so treat a gap of a few points as a tie. Then we divide each card's price by its score. That gives dollars per point, and lower is better. Prices change every day, so for every card you'll also get a walk-away price, the point where it stops being worth it.

The lineup has six cards. Intel's Arc B570 with 10 gigabytes of video memory, and the Arc B580 with 12. Nvidia's RTX 50 50 and RTX 50 60, both with 8 gigabytes. AMD's Radeon RX 90 60 XT, sold with either 8 or 16 gigabytes. And one more that keeps sneaking into Black Friday deals, the RTX 30 50 with 6 gigabytes. Let's start at the bottom.

Skip tier, card one: the RTX 30 50 6 gigabyte. It carries the same name as the 8 gigabyte RTX 30 50, but it has fewer cores, 2,304 instead of 2,560, on a narrower memory bus, so it moves 168 gigabytes per second instead of 224. It's built to draw just 70 watts, and early benchmarks put it about twenty percent behind the 8 gigabyte version, which was already the entry-level card of its generation. If a cheap prebuilt lists an RTX 30 50 with 6 gigabytes, that's where the money was saved.

Skip tier, card two, and this one surprises people: the RTX 50 50. It isn't a bad chip. It scores about 78, roughly the same as Intel's cheaper B570, and TechSpot measured the Arc B580 about fourteen percent faster across sixteen games. The problem is the price. It launched at 249 dollars, and in mid November Best Buy listed it at 400. That's about 5 dollars and 13 cents per point, with only 8 gigabytes of memory. Walk-away price: anything above what the B570 costs, because the B570 matches it and gives you 2 more gigabytes.

B tier: the RTX 50 60 and the 8 gigabyte RX 90 60 XT. Together they set our yardstick of 100. In TechPowerUp's testing the 16 gigabyte Radeon was about three and a half percent faster than the RTX 50 60 at 1080p, and the 8 gigabyte version about five percent slower than the 16, so these two are a tie. The RTX 50 60 launched at 299 dollars and was listed around 500 on Amazon in mid November. That's exactly 5 dollars per point. Both have 8 gigabytes, which still works at 1080p, but costs you real frames at 1440p with ultra textures. Verdict: if you want one of these, buy whichever is cheaper on the day, and don't pay more than 400.

Now the most interesting card in the list: the RX 90 60 XT with 16 gigabytes. It's the fastest card here, at about 105, and it's the cheapest way to get 16 gigabytes of video memory. It launched at 349 dollars. In mid November, Newegg listed one at 750. That's over 7 dollars per point, the worst value on this list, for the best card on this list. Great card, terrible price. Its walk-away price is 420 dollars. At or below that, it's the one I'd buy, because 16 gigabytes keeps it useful for years.

A tier: Intel's Arc B580. It scores about 88, so it's a bit slower than the RTX 50 60, but it has 12 gigabytes of memory. It launched at 249 dollars, and in November it's been averaging around 390 at US stores. That's about 4 dollars and 43 cents per point. Cheaper per frame than every Nvidia and AMD card on this list, with more memory than any 8 gigabyte card.

And S tier, the best value this Black Friday: Intel's Arc B570. It scores about 78, the same as the RTX 50 50, and in reviews it landed within about seven percent of the RTX 40 60 at 1080p. It has 10 gigabytes of memory. It launched at 219 dollars, and on November 18th Newegg listed it at 289. That works out to about 3 dollars and 71 cents per point, the lowest on the board. It costs 111 dollars less than the RTX 50 50 for the same speed.

But there's a catch, and it decides whether Intel is right for you. Arc cards need a feature called Resizable BAR switched on in your motherboard's settings, or they lose a lot of performance. And on older processors, Intel's driver has needed more CPU time than AMD's or Nvidia's. When TechSpot paired the B580 with a Ryzen 5 2600, it ran about twenty percent slower than it should have. Newer drivers improved this, but some games are still affected. So if your PC is several years old, or your board doesn't offer Resizable BAR, skip Arc and pick the RX 90 60 XT or the RTX 50 60 instead.

Here's how to judge any Black Friday deal in ten seconds. Aim for 4 dollars per point or less. That means the B570 is a good deal under about 310 dollars. The B580 under 350. The RTX 50 60 or the 8 gigabyte Radeon under 400. And the 16 gigabyte Radeon under 420. If a card misses its number, it isn't a deal, no matter what the banner says the original price was.

So here's the verdict. If your PC is from the last few years and you play at 1080p, buy the Arc B570. Step up to the B580 only if you find it under 350. Skip the RTX 50 50 and the 6 gigabyte RTX 30 50 at today's prices. And if a 16 gigabyte RX 90 60 XT ever drops near 420 dollars, buy it on the spot. It's the card that will last the longest.
